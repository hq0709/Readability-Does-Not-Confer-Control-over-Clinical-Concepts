Vision-language models (VLMs), both general-purpose and medical, now answer clinical questions about radiographs \citep{li2023llavamed,chen2024chexagent,sellergren2025medgemma}. As these models approach clinical use, interpretability methods are expected to make them auditable concept by concept. Linear probes test which concepts a model represents \citep{alain2017understanding,belinkov2022probing}, and activation steering adds a probe direction back to change the model's behavior \citep{turner2023steering,panickssery2024caa}. Probes already decode findings such as pleural effusion from these models \citep{zhu2026lost,pedersen2026probes}. Whether a decodable finding can also be controlled along its probe direction, however, remains an open question.

A case in Qwen2.5-VL-7B \citep{bai2025qwen25vl} on NIH ChestX-ray14 \citep{wang2017chestxray} shows why the question matters (Figure~\ref{fig:framework}b). Adding the Effusion probe direction to the visual representation raises the probability that the model answers yes to ``Is there pleural effusion?'' by $\cfSeedWEff$, above the random and sham references. By common steering criteria, the probe direction controls effusion. However, adding the lung-nodule direction to the same patients raises the effusion answer by $\cfSeedWNodule$. The direction that decodes effusion is thus not the one along which the model answers.

The combination of probing and steering presupposes that decodability along a direction implies control along it. Prior work questions this presupposition: information a probe recovers need not be used by the model \citep{ravichander2021probing}, vectors trained for separability can absorb distractor signals \citep{pahde2025navigating}, and the features that identify a concept largely differ from those controlling the output \citep{arad2025saes}. None tests whether a probe direction controls its own concept rather than a competing one, and steering evaluations cannot: random and sham references carry no label.

Testing the presupposition requires a labeled reference, namely the directions of the other concepts. We call a concept \emph{owned} when its own direction changes its own answer more than any direction fitted in the same manner for a competing concept. We formalize this comparison as RAW (Read--Answer--Write; Section~\ref{sec:framework}), an evaluation that grades each concept as readable by a probe, answerable by the model, and owned by its direction. Because RAW requires only a labeled concept set and an open-weight VLM, it applies unchanged to natural images; ownership of natural objects in the same checkpoints attributes a clinical failure to the model rather than to the method. We evaluate \cfCheckpoints{} checkpoints from \cfFamiliesWord{} families (3B--72B) on NIH ChestX-ray14 and CheXpert Plus \citep{chambon2024chexpertplus}, with COCO objects \citep{lin2014coco} as the natural-image control.

The effusion example is representative. Chest findings pass the read and answer grades about as often as natural objects, yet only \cfChestOwnedPct\% of chest cells are owned, compared with \cfCocoOwnedPct\% of object cells (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}). The gap holds in general-purpose, medical, and chest-specialized checkpoints from 3B to 72B, on both chest datasets, under alternative direction constructions, write sites, and radiologist labels, and among the concepts each model answers best. The gap is explained neither by difficulty nor by the vision encoder: harder COCO objects remain owned; checkpoints that share a vision tower own different findings; and non-clinical attributes of the same radiographs share the deficit. The model instead answers along a direction fitted to its own answers and nearly orthogonal to the probe direction on chest radiographs. Decodability is thus a property of the probe, whereas control is a property of the model that reads the representation. Steering claims built on probe directions, medical or not, require the competitor comparison.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item We identify a gap between reading and controlling clinical concepts in medical VLMs: readable, answerable findings are rarely owned by their probe directions, unlike natural objects.
\item We introduce RAW, an evaluation that separates reading a concept from writing it by scoring each probe direction against directions fitted the same way for the competing concepts, and release it with scorecards and write matrices.
\item Across \cfCells{} checkpoint--concept cells and about 30 million scored answers, controlling for difficulty, direction construction, write site, and label source, we trace the deficit to the reader, which answers along a direction orthogonal to the probe's.
\end{itemize}

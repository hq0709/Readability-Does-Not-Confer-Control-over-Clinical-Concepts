Vision-language models (VLMs) answer questions about images from everyday photographs to medical scans, and interpretability aims to audit them concept by concept: a probe decodes a concept along a direction, and steering adds that direction back. Does decodability along a direction imply control along it? On chest radiographs, adding the nodule direction can raise the model's effusion answer more than adding the effusion direction does, which standard checks against unlabeled random directions miss. We introduce RAW (Read--Answer--Write), an evaluation that grades each concept as readable by a probe, answerable by the model, and \emph{owned} when its own direction, rather than a competing concept's, controls its answer. Across \cfCheckpoints{} general-purpose and medical checkpoints, findings on NIH ChestX-ray14 and CheXpert Plus are readable and answerable, yet \cfChestOwnedPct{}\% of checkpoint--concept cells are owned, against \cfCocoOwnedPct{}\% for COCO objects. The gap persists among the best-answered concepts, under alternative directions and expert labels, and for non-clinical radiograph attributes. Checkpoints with identical vision towers own different concepts, pointing to the connector and language model, and a direction fitted to the model's answers is owned more often yet nearly orthogonal to the probe direction on chest radiographs. Decodability is not enough for control; steering claims need the competitor comparison.

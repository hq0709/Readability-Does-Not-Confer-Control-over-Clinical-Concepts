Probing and activation steering are routinely combined on the assumption that a concept decodable along a direction can be controlled along it. In medical vision-language models (VLMs), this would let a model be audited and corrected finding by finding, but the standard check against random and sham directions cannot test it. We introduce RAW (Read--Answer--Write), an evaluation that grades each concept as readable by a probe, answerable by the model, and \emph{owned} by its direction, meaning that its direction moves its answer more than any competing concept's direction. Across \cfCheckpoints{} general-purpose and medical checkpoints (3B--72B), clinical findings on NIH ChestX-ray14 and CheXpert Plus are readable in \cfChestReadablePct{}\% of checkpoint--concept cells and answerable in \cfChestAnswerablePct{}\%, yet owned in only \cfChestOwnedPct{}\%, while COCO objects are owned in \cfCocoOwnedPct{}\%. The gap persists among the cells the model answers best (\cfStratChestTopPct{}\% against \cfStratNatTopPct{}\%), under alternative directions, write sites, and radiologist labels, and extends to non-clinical attributes of the same radiographs. The gap lies in the model that reads the representation: checkpoints sharing a vision tower, and thus the same write vectors, own different concepts. The model instead answers along a direction fitted to its own answers, nearly orthogonal to the probe direction on chest radiographs. Decodability is not enough for control; steering claims need the competitor comparison.

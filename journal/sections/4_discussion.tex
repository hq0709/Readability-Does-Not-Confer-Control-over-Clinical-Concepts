\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:implications}

\subsection{Principal findings}

On chest radiographs, \jnTwoWayChestPct{}\% of checkpoint--concept pairs passed both tests of selective control, compared with \jnTwoWayCocoPct{}\% on COCO. Ownership remained less common on chest radiographs at comparable answer difficulty. Differences between models that shared the same vision tower showed that the reader affected which findings a direction could control.

Ravichander et al.\cite{ravichander2021probing} showed that a probe can recover information the model leaves unused. Arad et al.\cite{arad2025saes} distinguished features that detect a concept from those that control its output. Haufe et al.\cite{haufe2014interpretation} distinguished classifier weights from activation patterns. Marks and Tegmark\cite{marks2024geometry} found that directions based on differences between class means controlled truth judgments more reliably than probe directions. RAW extends this work by testing a direction against other labeled findings and measuring its effects across all their answers.

\subsection{Clinical implications}
\label{sec:clinical-implications}

A concept-level explanation can point a radiologist to the wrong finding. In the effusion experiment, a probe detected information about effusion and the effusion direction raised the model's yes score for that finding by \cfSeedWEff{}. Taken together, those observations could make it seem that the model used effusion to reach its answer. Yet the direction learned for a nodule raised the same answer by \cfSeedWNodule{}. An explanation that names effusion on the strength of the first comparison would give the radiologist a reason to trust the answer without showing that the response was specific to effusion. Such a rationale can be more misleading than a bare answer. It invites automation bias by giving the reader an apparent basis for accepting the model's suggestion. A false-positive answer can look justified, and a correct answer can appear to rest on evidence for the wrong finding.

The same problem creates a safety risk when an edit is meant to correct a single finding. Among chest directions that passed the random and sham checks, the sum of absolute changes on other answers was a median of \jnOffTargetChest{} times the intended effect. The corresponding ratio on COCO was \jnOffTargetCoco{}. A team proposing to suppress false-positive effusion by reversing its probe direction would need to test that edit across the other findings before using it on reports. In the chest evaluations, a change intended for one finding could also shift answers the team had no intention of changing. If those answers inform a draft report, checking only for the disappearance of the effusion statement could leave changes elsewhere in the report undetected. A finding-level edit is therefore a change to the whole report, including findings that appeared correct before the edit.

Standard checks can give a false sense of selectivity. Random and sham controls passed \jnRefChest{} chest directions. Of these, \jnRefNotTwoWayChestPct{}\% then failed a comparison with labeled findings. An audit that stopped at the standard checks would record those directions as passing and give the clinical team no way to see which ones also moved a different answer or lost to another finding's direction. Likewise, probe accuracy tells reviewers how well a finding can be read from the features. Answer accuracy tells them how well the model responds before an edit. Model cards and regulatory documentation should report those measures for what they assess. A claim that an edit targets a particular finding needs evidence from the competing directions and from every answer evaluated in the same test.

Medical and medically fine-tuned VLMs are not reliable sources of finding-level evidence on chest radiographs. The medically tuned group included MedGemma 4B and 27B, Lingshu 7B and 32B, LLaVA-Med, and HuatuoGPT-Vision-7B. Its finding directions owned only \cfSpecMedOwned{} of \cfSpecMedN{} chest cells. The group read \cfSpecMedReadable{} and answered \cfSpecMedAnswerable{} of those cells. The chest-trained towers in CheXagent-2-3B, CheXagent-8B, and LLaVA-Rad-7B achieved a median probe AUROC of \cfSpecTowerProbeAuroc{} on NIH, compared with \cfSpecRestProbeAuroc{} for the other towers. The chest-trained group owned only \cfSpecTowerOwned{} of \cfSpecTowerN{} chest cells. It read \cfSpecTowerReadable{} and answered \cfSpecTowerAnswerable{} of those cells.

Both medical groups had higher ownership rates than the general-purpose group, which owned \cfSpecGenOwned{} of \cfSpecGenN{} chest cells. Each medical group owned fewer than one in four chest cells, well below the \cfCocoOwnedPct{}\% ownership rate on COCO. Larger checkpoints owned more findings in some families (Fig.~\ref{fig:ladders}), so a hospital evaluating an upgrade should ask for evidence on the deployed checkpoint and each finding it will handle. Medical fine-tuning and chest-specific pretraining leave a wide gap between answering a question and selectively controlling its answer. High probe or answer accuracy alone gives too little evidence to trust a finding-level explanation or correction.

Until selective control is demonstrated for each finding, a medical VLM should not be used as a source of diagnostic evidence, explanation, or finding-level correction in clinical practice. The evaluation should report the write matrix, the effects of competing directions on each target, and the effects of each edit on other findings. The same test on natural images shows that the procedure can detect selective control where it occurs. The radiologist should remain responsible for deciding whether each finding is present. Any finding-level edit should trigger review of the full report before the changed text enters the clinical record.

\subsection{Why the label direction fails on radiographs}

Readers given identical image features and edit directions differed in ownership. The language model reproduced the chest and COCO pattern even when it received the edit after the connector. On chest radiographs, the label direction and the direction fitted to the model's own answers were nearly perpendicular, with a median cosine of \cfAnsCosChestMed{}. Non-clinical attributes were readily decoded and showed similarly low ownership. These observations are consistent with readers responding to signals shared across multiple questions about a radiograph. A direction learned from report labels can therefore pick up information that does little to control the corresponding answer.

\subsection{Recommendations for evaluating medical VLMs}

Evaluations of medical VLMs should report the full write matrix for each checkpoint and dataset, the rank of each finding's own direction against the alternatives on its answer, and the absolute changes it produces on the other answers, together with random and sham references and a natural-image control. These tests should be repeated after every model update or change to the edit.

\subsection{Scope}
\label{sec:limitations}

This study used frontal chest radiographs with report-derived labels from NIH ChestX-ray14 and CheXpert Plus, supplemented by a CheXpert consensus-label analysis, and COCO photographs as a control. RAW applies to open-weight VLMs whose activations can be accessed. The grades characterize short answers under the edits and labeled concepts evaluated here.
